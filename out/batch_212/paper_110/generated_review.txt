**Review:**

This paper addresses an important and practical problem in causal inference: how to select among competing CATE estimators when ground-truth counterfactual outcomes are unobserved. The authors conduct an extensive empirical benchmark of existing surrogate model selection metrics (e.g., those based on orthogonalization, influence functions, or causal forests) and propose novel strategies centered on hyperparameter tuning and causal ensembling. The evaluation uses AutoML for fair hyperparameter selection and incorporates realistic datasets via generative modeling to provide detailed empirical trends.

**Strengths:**
1. **Addresses a genuine gap:** Model selection for CATE estimation is under-studied compared to standard supervised learning, and the lack of a cross-validation analogue is a real practical bottleneck. A systematic empirical comparison is valuable.
2. **Methodological rigor:** The use of AutoML to tune hyperparameters associated with surrogate metrics ensures fairer comparison than ad-hoc tuning, and the incorporation of generative modeling to create realistic datasets strengthens the empirical analysis.
3. **Practical relevance:** The proposed strategies based on careful hyperparameter selection and causal ensembling are actionable and could guide practitioners.

**Weaknesses:**
1. **Limited novelty in methods:** The paper is primarily an empirical study; while novel surrogate metrics are mentioned, the abstract does not clearly articulate what these are or how they differ fundamentally from existing approaches. The proposed strategies (hyperparameter tuning, ensembling) are relatively incremental.
2. **Insufficient detail on scope and findings:** The abstract does not specify which surrogate metrics are compared, how many datasets are used, what the key empirical findings are, or which proposed strategies emerge as most effective. This makes it difficult to assess the significance of the contributions.
3. **Unclear generalization:** It is unclear whether findings hold across different causal inference settings (e.g., observational vs. experimental, linear vs. nonlinear effects, different covariate distributions) or are specific to the datasets and estimators tested.
4. **Missing theoretical insight:** The paper appears to be purely empirical; there is no discussion of why certain metrics work better or under what conditions, limiting the conceptual contribution.

**Questions:**
- Which novel surrogate metrics are introduced, and what is their theoretical or intuitive motivation?
- What are the most significant empirical findings? Do any surrogate metrics consistently outperform others, or does performance depend heavily on the data-generating process?
- How sensitive are the results to the choice of CATE estimators and hyperparameter ranges explored?
- Can you provide guidance on which metric(s) practitioners should use in different settings?

**Overall score:**
5: marginally below the acceptance threshold

This is a solid empirical study that tackles a practical problem, but it falls short of the novelty and insight expected at a top venue. The contributions are primarily empirical benchmarking with incremental methodological proposals (hyperparameter tuning, ensembling). Without clearer articulation of novel metrics, deeper analysis of *why* certain approaches work, or stronger empirical findings that substantially change practice, the work reads more as a thorough experimental report than a research contribution with lasting impact. A venue focused on empirical causal inference or a workshop might be better suited; for ICLR, the paper would benefit from either stronger methodological innovation or more surprising and well-explained empirical insights.
