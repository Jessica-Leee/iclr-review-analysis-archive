**Review:**

This paper proposes SharpeRatio@k, a new evaluation metric for off-policy evaluation (OPE) estimators that measures risk-return tradeoff and efficiency of policy portfolios under varying online evaluation budgets. The key insight is that existing OPE metrics focus narrowly on accuracy or downstream policy selection, overlooking the practical concern of efficiency during online deployment. By drawing inspiration from portfolio evaluation in finance, the authors introduce a metric that captures both the expected return and variance of policy performance across the top-k candidates selected by an OPE estimator, enabling a more holistic assessment of OPE methods.

**Strengths:**
- The motivation is well-articulated and practically relevant: OPE is often used to identify promising policies for online A/B testing, yet existing metrics do not directly measure the risk-return efficiency of this downstream deployment process.
- The proposed SharpeRatio@k metric is intuitive and grounded in established financial theory, making it interpretable and easy to communicate to practitioners.
- The authors provide concrete examples demonstrating that SharpeRatio@k can distinguish between conservative and high-stakes OPE estimators where existing metrics produce degenerate results, suggesting the metric captures meaningful differences.
- The paper includes an open-source implementation and a benchmark study across multiple OPE estimators, supporting reproducibility and practical adoption.

**Weaknesses:**
- The abstract and summary lack sufficient technical detail about how SharpeRatio@k is formally defined. It is unclear how the metric aggregates risk and return across different values of k, or how it handles the variance estimation in offline settings where only a single offline dataset is available.
- The paper mentions "two example scenarios" but provides no details on their setup, scale, or results. Without concrete experimental results in the abstract or summary, it is difficult to assess the empirical validity of the proposed metric or the quality of the benchmark findings.
- The connection between portfolio theory and OPE policy selection could be made more rigorous. It is not obvious that the Sharpe ratio, designed for financial assets with known return distributions, directly transfers to the OPE setting where estimates are noisy and derived from offline data.
- The paper does not discuss potential limitations or failure modes of SharpeRatio@k. For instance, how sensitive is the metric to the choice of k, or to the offline data distribution? How does it perform when the offline data is highly biased?

**Questions:**
- How is the variance term in SharpeRatio@k estimated from offline data? Is it based on the empirical variance of policy returns across the top-k candidates, or does it incorporate uncertainty in the OPE estimates themselves?
- Can you provide more details on the benchmark experiments? How many OPE estimators were compared, and on how many domains or datasets?
- How does SharpeRatio@k compare to simpler alternatives, such as the expected regret of the top-k policies or a risk-adjusted return metric based on quantiles?

**Overall score:**
6: marginally above the acceptance threshold

The paper addresses a genuine gap in OPE evaluation by proposing a metric that captures practical deployment efficiency. The motivation is sound and the proposed solution is intuitive. However, the abstract and summary lack sufficient technical and empirical detail to fully validate the contribution. The benchmark study and open-source implementation are valuable, but their results are not described. With more complete presentation of the methodology and experimental findings, this could be a solid contribution to the OPE literature.
