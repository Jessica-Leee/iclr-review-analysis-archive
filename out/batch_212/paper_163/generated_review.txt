**Review:**

This paper proposes COP (Competition Over Pixel features), a method for object-centric learning that aims to discover abstract slot representations from images without supervision. The approach is conceptually straightforward: it encodes an image via CNN, applies alternating convolution and MaxPool layers to create competition and extract primitive slots, then uses these slots as queries in a Cross-Attention mechanism over the encoded image. The authors claim their method is fully-differentiable, non-iterative, scalable, and implementable with standard layers (convolution, MaxPool, attention), while achieving competitive or superior performance on standard benchmarks.

**Strengths:**
- **Simplicity and implementability**: The method's reliance on standard, differentiable components (convolution, MaxPool, attention) is a genuine practical advantage over more complex or non-differentiable alternatives. This makes it accessible and reproducible.
- **Scalability and efficiency**: The non-iterative design and avoidance of iterative refinement (common in slot-based methods) should yield computational benefits, though empirical timing comparisons are not mentioned in the abstract.
- **Competitive empirical results**: The claim of matching or exceeding prior methods on standard benchmarks suggests the simplicity does not come at a significant performance cost.
- **Code availability**: Public release enhances reproducibility and impact.

**Weaknesses:**
- **Limited novelty in components**: While the overall combination is simple, the individual components (CNN encoding, MaxPool-based competition, cross-attention) are well-established. The conceptual novelty—how competition via MaxPool discovers meaningful slots—is not clearly articulated in the abstract.
- **Insufficient detail on the mechanism**: The abstract does not explain *why* alternating convolution and MaxPool layers should discover semantically meaningful slots, or how the "competition" mechanism ensures disentanglement or object discovery. This is a critical gap for understanding the contribution.
- **Benchmark evaluation unclear**: The abstract states results are "competitive or outperforms previous methods" but provides no specific numbers, datasets, or baselines. Without concrete comparisons (e.g., vs. MONet, IODINE, Slot Attention), it is difficult to assess the actual empirical contribution.
- **Missing ablations and analysis**: No mention of ablation studies (e.g., importance of MaxPool vs. convolution, sensitivity to architecture choices) or qualitative analysis (e.g., visualizations of discovered slots, failure cases).

**Questions:**
- How does the "competition" mechanism via MaxPool differ fundamentally from existing pooling-based or attention-based slot discovery methods?
- What specific benchmarks and baselines were used for evaluation, and what are the quantitative improvements?
- How does performance scale with the number of objects or image complexity?
- Are there ablation studies isolating the contribution of each component (convolution, MaxPool, cross-attention)?

**Overall score:**
6: marginally above the acceptance threshold

The paper presents a genuinely simple and practical method with claimed competitive results, which is valuable for the object-centric learning community. However, the abstract lacks sufficient detail on the mechanism, novelty, and empirical validation to make a strong case. The work appears sound in principle but needs clearer exposition of why the approach works and concrete benchmark comparisons to justify acceptance. If the full paper provides these details and the empirical results are indeed strong, this could be a solid contribution.
