**Review:**

This paper provides a theoretical framework for understanding in-context reinforcement learning (ICRL) via supervised pretraining of transformers. The authors analyze two training methods—algorithm distillation and decision-pretrained transformers—and prove that under model realizability, a supervised-pretrained transformer will imitate the conditional expectation of an expert algorithm given observed trajectories, with generalization error scaling with model capacity and a distribution divergence factor. The key contribution is showing that transformers with ReLU attention can efficiently approximate near-optimal online RL algorithms (LinUCB, Thompson sampling for stochastic linear bandits; UCB-VI for tabular MDPs), providing the first quantitative analysis of ICRL capabilities from offline pretraining.

**Strengths:**
- **Novel theoretical contribution**: The paper addresses an important gap by providing formal analysis of when and how transformers can perform ICRL, moving beyond empirical observations to quantitative guarantees.
- **Concrete algorithmic results**: Demonstrating that transformers can approximate specific, well-studied RL algorithms (LinUCB, Thompson sampling, UCB-VI) is a meaningful result that connects transformer expressiveness to classical RL theory.
- **Principled framework**: The analysis of supervised pretraining under model realizability and the characterization of generalization error via distribution divergence provides a structured perspective on the problem.
- **Relevance**: The work addresses a timely question about large pretrained models' capabilities in sequential decision-making, with clear implications for understanding and designing better ICRL systems.

**Weaknesses:**
- **Model realizability assumption**: The assumption that the transformer can perfectly represent the expert algorithm is strong and may not hold in practice, especially for complex algorithms. The paper does not discuss how violations of this assumption affect the results or provide empirical validation of this assumption.
- **Limited empirical validation**: The abstract does not mention experiments validating the theoretical predictions. It is unclear whether the bounds are tight, whether the distribution divergence factor is empirically significant, or how the theory translates to practical performance on real offline RL datasets.
- **Scope of algorithms**: While LinUCB, Thompson sampling, and UCB-VI are classical, the restriction to these specific algorithms and the linear/tabular settings limits the scope. It is unclear how the framework extends to more complex function approximation or modern deep RL algorithms.
- **Presentation of technical content**: The abstract provides limited detail on the proof techniques, the form of the distribution divergence factor, or the capacity-dependent bounds. This makes it difficult to assess the tightness and practical implications of the results without the full paper.

**Questions:**
- How does the distribution divergence factor scale with problem parameters (e.g., dimension, horizon, number of actions)? Is it polynomial or exponential?
- Can you provide empirical evidence that the model realizability assumption holds for transformers trained on offline RL data, or characterize when it breaks down?
- How do the theoretical bounds compare to empirical generalization errors on standard offline RL benchmarks?
- Does the framework extend to function approximation beyond linear bandits and tabular MDPs?

**Overall score:**
6: marginally above the acceptance threshold

The paper makes a solid theoretical contribution to understanding ICRL by providing the first quantitative analysis of transformer expressiveness for approximating classical RL algorithms. The framework is principled and addresses a relevant question. However, the strong model realizability assumption, lack of empirical validation in the abstract, and limited scope to classical algorithms prevent this from being a strong accept. The work would be strengthened by experiments validating the theory and discussing the practical implications of the bounds.
