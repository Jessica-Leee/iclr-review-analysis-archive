**Review:**

This paper proposes PASS (Pruning with Almost-Sure Sparsity targets), a framework for pruning redundant attention heads in Transformer architectures. The key innovation is the introduction of "almost-sure sparsity" targets to address training-testing inconsistency and hard structural constraints in prior work. The authors further develop PASSCONC by incorporating a "concentrator" technique to improve efficiency. The approach is evaluated on encoder-decoder Transformers (IWSLT14 translation) and BERT (GLUE), claiming improvements of up to 1.33 BLEU, 1.44% accuracy, and 60% attention layer speedups over prior work.

**Strengths:**
- The motivation is clear: existing pruning methods suffer from either training-testing mismatch or overly restrictive structural constraints. The notion of almost-sure sparsity appears designed to address this gap.
- Evaluation spans two important architectures (encoder-decoder and BERT) and two diverse tasks (machine translation and NLU), suggesting generality.
- Reported improvements are substantial (1.33 BLEU, 1.44% accuracy, 60% speedup), which would be significant if reproducible.
- The paper tackles a practically important problem—attention head pruning is relevant for deployment efficiency.

**Weaknesses:**
- The abstract provides no formal definition or intuition for "almost-sure sparsity." Without understanding the core technical contribution, it is impossible to assess novelty or soundness. What distinguishes this from existing sparsity-inducing regularization or stochastic pruning methods?
- The "concentrator" technique is mentioned but not explained. Its design, motivation, and role in the framework remain opaque from the abstract alone.
- No comparison details are given: which SOTA baselines are outperformed? Are comparisons fair (same training budget, hyperparameter tuning)? The claim of "60% higher attention layer speedups" is vague—speedup relative to what?
- The abstract lacks ablation studies or analysis of which components (almost-sure sparsity vs. concentrator) drive the gains, making it difficult to assess individual contributions.

**Questions:**
- Can you provide a formal definition of "almost-sure sparsity" and explain how it differs from existing probabilistic or stochastic pruning approaches?
- What is the computational overhead of the PASS framework during training? Does the reported speedup account for this?
- How sensitive is the method to hyperparameters? Are the improvements consistent across different random seeds?
- Can you clarify the speedup metric: is it wall-clock time, FLOPs, or memory? On what hardware?

**Overall score:**
5: marginally below the acceptance threshold

The paper addresses a relevant problem and reports strong empirical results, but the abstract is too vague to assess the technical contribution. The core concepts—almost-sure sparsity and the concentrator—are not explained, making it impossible to evaluate novelty or soundness. A full paper review would be needed, but based on the abstract alone, the lack of clarity on the method itself is a significant concern for a venue like ICLR that values technical rigor and reproducibility.
