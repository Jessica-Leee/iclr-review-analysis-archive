**Review:**

This paper proposes a confidence-driven sampling strategy for backdoor attacks on DNNs. The core contribution is a straightforward method that selects poisoned samples with lower confidence scores—targeting samples near the model's decision boundary—rather than using random sampling. The authors argue this approach makes backdoor attacks more robust against defense mechanisms and claim the method is agnostic to trigger design, offering compatibility with various existing backdoor attack techniques.

**Strengths:**

- **Clear motivation**: The observation that random sampling of poisoned samples is detectable and defensible is intuitive, and targeting low-confidence samples near decision boundaries is a reasonable heuristic.
- **Method simplicity and generality**: The confidence-driven sampling approach is straightforward to implement and claimed to be independent of trigger design, potentially applicable across different backdoor attack methods.
- **Empirical validation**: The paper includes comprehensive experiments demonstrating the effectiveness of the approach against defense strategies.

**Weaknesses:**

- **Limited novelty**: Selecting hard examples (low-confidence samples) for training is a well-established principle in machine learning (e.g., curriculum learning, hard negative mining). The paper does not clearly articulate what is novel beyond applying this known principle to backdoor attacks.
- **Insufficient technical depth**: The abstract provides no algorithmic details, theoretical justification, or analysis of why confidence-driven sampling specifically enhances robustness against defenses. The mechanism by which this strategy defeats detection remains unclear.
- **Incomplete experimental context**: The abstract mentions "comprehensive empirical experiments" but provides no details on datasets, baselines, defense methods tested, or quantitative results. Without these specifics, it is impossible to assess the magnitude of improvement or the validity of claims.
- **Missing related work and positioning**: The paper does not discuss how this approach relates to existing work on adversarial training, sample selection in poisoning attacks, or defenses against backdoor attacks. The novelty relative to prior art is not established.

**Questions:**

1. How does confidence-driven sampling differ fundamentally from existing hard example mining or curriculum learning approaches applied to poisoning attacks?
2. What specific defense mechanisms are evaluated, and by how much does the proposed method improve robustness compared to random sampling baselines?
3. Does the method require access to model confidence scores during the poisoning phase? If so, what are the practical implications?
4. How sensitive is the approach to the choice of confidence threshold or sampling strategy?

**Overall score:**
4: below the acceptance threshold. While the core idea has merit, the paper lacks sufficient novelty, technical depth, and experimental detail to meet the standards of a top-tier venue. The contribution reads as an incremental application of a known principle rather than a significant methodological advance. Substantial revision with detailed experiments, theoretical analysis, and clearer positioning relative to related work would be needed.
