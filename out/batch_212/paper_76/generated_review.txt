**Review:**

SetCSE proposes a framework that applies set-theoretic operations to sentence embeddings for complex information retrieval. The core idea is to represent query semantics as sets and introduce an inter-set contrastive learning objective to improve sentence embedding models' discriminatory capability. The authors claim their approach enables retrieval tasks with compound prompts (e.g., intersection, difference, operation series) that existing methods cannot handle, and that SetCSE adheres to conventions of human language for compounded semantics.

**Strengths:**
- The conceptual framing of set operations for structured querying is intuitive and potentially useful for complex retrieval scenarios.
- The paper identifies a real gap: existing sentence embedding methods do not naturally support set-theoretic operations on queries.
- The inter-set contrastive learning objective is a reasonable approach to encode set semantics into embeddings.

**Weaknesses:**
- The abstract provides no concrete experimental results, baselines, or datasets. Without empirical validation, it is impossible to assess whether SetCSE actually outperforms existing methods or whether the claimed capabilities are realized in practice.
- The paper lacks clarity on key technical details: How exactly are sets represented in the embedding space? How does the inter-set contrastive loss differ from standard contrastive objectives? What is the precise formulation of intersection, difference, and operation series?
- No discussion of related work or positioning relative to existing retrieval frameworks (e.g., dense retrieval, neural ranking, or other structured query methods) is provided in the abstract.
- The claim that SetCSE "adheres to conventions of human language expressions" is vague and unsupported without linguistic or empirical evidence.

**Questions:**
- What are the concrete experimental results on standard benchmarks (e.g., MS MARCO, Natural Questions, or similar)?
- How does SetCSE compare quantitatively to strong baselines such as DPR, ColBERT, or other recent dense retrieval methods?
- Can you provide the mathematical formulation of the inter-set contrastive loss and the set operations (intersection, difference)?
- On what datasets and tasks is the method evaluated, and what types of compound queries are tested?

**Overall score:**
3: reject, not good enough

The paper presents an interesting conceptual idea but provides no empirical evidence, technical depth, or experimental validation in the abstract. For a venue like ICLR, a submission must demonstrate clear methodological novelty and convincing experimental results. The abstract alone is insufficient to evaluate soundness or impact, and the lack of concrete details, baselines, and results makes it impossible to recommend acceptance.
