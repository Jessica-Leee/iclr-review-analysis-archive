**Review:**

This paper proposes augmenting CLIP training with pseudo-labels generated by task-specific vision models from model zoos to improve visual representations across multiple downstream tasks. The method is straightforward: open-source task-specific models generate pseudo-labels on an uncurated, noisy image-text dataset, and CLIP is trained jointly on contrastive image-text pairs and these pseudo-labeled tasks (segmentation, detection, depth estimation, surface normal estimation). The authors report substantial improvements of up to 16.3% across these vision tasks while maintaining CLIP's zero-shot classification and promptability.

**Strengths:**
- **Practical and simple approach**: The core idea of leveraging readily available model zoo experts to generate pseudo-supervision is intuitive and easy to implement, making it accessible for practitioners.
- **Comprehensive evaluation**: The paper demonstrates improvements across diverse downstream tasks (segmentation, detection, depth, surface normals), suggesting the approach generalizes beyond a single domain.
- **Preservation of existing capabilities**: The claim that CLIP's zero-shot classification and promptability are maintained is important and, if validated, shows the method does not sacrifice the model's original strengths.
- **Substantial empirical gains**: Improvements of up to 16.3% are non-trivial and suggest meaningful benefits from the pseudo-supervision strategy.

**Weaknesses:**
- **Limited novelty**: The core contribution—using task-specific models to generate pseudo-labels for multi-task learning—is relatively incremental. Multi-task learning with pseudo-labels is well-established; the novelty lies primarily in applying it to CLIP, which is somewhat narrow.
- **Insufficient methodological detail**: The abstract provides no information on how pseudo-labels are generated, how noisy labels are handled, how the multi-task loss is weighted, or how the method scales with dataset size. These details are critical for reproducibility and understanding the approach's robustness.
- **Unclear experimental setup**: The abstract does not specify which model zoo experts are used, how they were selected, whether results are averaged over multiple runs, or what the baseline comparisons are. Without these details, it is difficult to assess the significance of the reported improvements.
- **Potential circular dependency**: Using task-specific models to improve CLIP on those same tasks risks inflating performance through label leakage or overfitting to the pseudo-label distribution, rather than learning genuinely improved visual representations. This concern is not addressed.
- **Missing ablations and analysis**: There is no discussion of which tasks contribute most to improvements, whether all model zoo experts are equally helpful, or how performance degrades with pseudo-label noise. Such analyses would strengthen the contribution.

**Questions:**
- How are pseudo-labels generated when the dataset is uncurated and noisy? Are there filtering or confidence thresholding mechanisms?
- Which specific model zoo experts are used, and how were they selected?
- How is the multi-task loss weighted relative to the contrastive loss, and was this tuned?
- Are the improvements on downstream tasks due to better visual features or simply task-specific overfitting to the pseudo-labels?
- How does performance vary with the quality and diversity of the model zoo experts used?

**Overall score:**
5: marginally below the acceptance threshold

The paper addresses a practical problem and reports strong empirical results, but the contribution is incremental (applying existing multi-task learning with pseudo-labels to CLIP) and the presentation lacks sufficient methodological detail. The core concern—whether improvements reflect genuinely better visual representations or task-specific overfitting to pseudo-labels—is not adequately addressed. For a venue like ICLR, the work would benefit from deeper analysis, clearer exposition of the method, and more rigorous experimental validation (e.g., ablations, sensitivity analyses, and evaluation on held-out downstream tasks not used to generate pseudo-labels).
