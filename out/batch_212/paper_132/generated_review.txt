**Review:**

This paper addresses an important gap in federated bandit learning by proposing Truth-FedBan, an incentive-compatible communication protocol that prevents strategic clients from misreporting their participation costs. The core contribution is demonstrating that incentive compatibility and sub-linear regret can be achieved simultaneously in federated bandit settings—a claim the authors present as novel. The proposed mechanism ensures that truthful cost reporting is a dominant strategy for clients while maintaining the same regret and communication guarantees as prior work that naively assumed truthfulness.

**Strengths:**

1. **Well-motivated problem:** The paper identifies a genuine vulnerability in existing federated bandit mechanisms—the assumption that clients truthfully report costs—and provides concrete motivation for why strategic misreporting is a natural concern in practice.

2. **Strong conceptual claim:** Demonstrating that incentive compatibility can be achieved without sacrificing regret or communication efficiency is a meaningful theoretical contribution. The claim that this is achieved "without any overhead" is noteworthy if substantiated.

3. **Clear presentation of the core idea:** The abstract effectively communicates the key insight: incentives should be independent of self-reported cost, making truthful reporting optimal.

**Weaknesses:**

1. **Limited detail on mechanism design:** The abstract provides no specifics on how Truth-FedBan actually constructs incentives to be independent of reported costs while maintaining optimality. The mechanism design approach and its theoretical justification remain opaque.

2. **Incomplete theoretical analysis:** The abstract claims sub-linear regret and communication cost guarantees but provides no regret bounds, convergence rates, or formal theorems. Without explicit rates (e.g., O(√T) or O(log T)), the claim of "nearly optimal" is unsubstantiated.

3. **Experimental validation unclear:** The abstract mentions "extensive numerical studies" but provides no details on datasets, baselines, or metrics. It is impossible to assess whether experiments validate the theoretical claims or compare fairly against existing incentivized federated bandit methods.

4. **Missing related work context:** The abstract does not clarify what "recent advances" in incentivized federated bandits exist or how Truth-FedBan differs mechanically from prior work beyond the incentive compatibility property.

**Questions:**

1. What is the explicit form of the incentive function in Truth-FedBan, and how does it ensure truthfulness while remaining independent of reported costs?
2. What are the concrete regret and communication complexity bounds? How do they compare quantitatively to prior work?
3. How does the mechanism handle clients with heterogeneous cost distributions, and does it scale to large numbers of clients?
4. What baselines were used in experiments, and on what datasets? How sensitive is the method to parameter choices?

**Overall score:**
6: marginally above the acceptance threshold

The paper tackles a relevant problem and claims a meaningful theoretical contribution (simultaneous incentive compatibility and near-optimal regret). However, the abstract lacks sufficient technical depth to fully evaluate the novelty and correctness of the approach. The mechanism design details, formal regret bounds, and experimental validation are all absent or vague. For acceptance, the full paper would need to provide rigorous proofs, explicit mechanism construction, and comprehensive experiments that clearly demonstrate the claimed advantages. The core idea is sound and timely, but the presentation here is too preliminary to assess impact confidently.
