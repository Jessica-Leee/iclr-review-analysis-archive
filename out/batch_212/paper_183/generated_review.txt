**Review:**

This paper introduces GPSE (graph positional and structural encoder), a learnable encoder designed to capture rich positional and structural encoding (PSE) representations that can augment any GNN architecture. The core contribution is training a single encoder that learns a common latent representation for multiple PSEs, with the key claim that this encoder is highly transferable across datasets from different distributions and modalities. The authors evaluate GPSE on a range of benchmarks and report improvements in certain tasks while achieving comparable performance to explicitly computed PSEs in others.

**Strengths:**
- **Addresses a relevant problem**: PSEs are acknowledged as essential for modern GNNs and graph Transformers, and a learnable, transferable approach to capturing them is a natural and potentially impactful direction.
- **Transferability claim**: The assertion that an encoder trained on one dataset can transfer to significantly different distributions and modalities is ambitious and, if well-validated, would be a valuable contribution.
- **Broad applicability**: The method is designed to augment any GNN, suggesting generality and practical utility.

**Weaknesses:**
- **Insufficient methodological detail**: The abstract provides no concrete description of the encoder architecture, training procedure, loss function, or how it learns "a common latent representation for multiple PSEs." Without these details, it is impossible to assess the technical soundness or novelty of the approach.
- **Vague empirical validation**: The paper claims improvements "in certain tasks" and parity "in other cases," but provides no specific results, datasets, baselines, or quantitative comparisons. The abstract does not specify which benchmarks were used, what the magnitude of improvements is, or how performance compares to state-of-the-art PSE methods.
- **Unclear novelty and positioning**: The relationship to existing self-supervised pre-training approaches is mentioned but not clarified. It is unclear what makes GPSE fundamentally different from or superior to existing pre-training methods for graph representations.
- **Missing ablations and analysis**: There is no discussion of ablation studies, failure cases, or analysis of what the encoder learns or why it transfers across modalities. The claim of transferability is central but entirely unsupported in the abstract.

**Questions:**
- What is the architecture of GPSE and how does it jointly learn multiple PSE representations?
- What are the specific datasets and benchmarks used, and what are the quantitative improvements over baselines?
- How does GPSE compare empirically to explicitly computed PSEs (e.g., Laplacian eigenvectors, random walk-based encodings) and to recent self-supervised pre-training methods?
- What evidence supports the claim of cross-modality transferability, and what are the failure modes?

**Overall score:**
3: Reject, not good enough. While the problem is relevant and the transferability idea is interesting, the abstract lacks sufficient methodological detail, concrete empirical results, and clarity to evaluate the contribution. The paper reads as a position statement rather than a complete research contribution. A full paper with detailed methodology, comprehensive experiments, and clear comparisons to baselines would be needed to assess this work properly.
