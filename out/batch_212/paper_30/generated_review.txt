**Review:**

This paper proposes augmented RGB (aRGB), a learned latent color space derived from an autoencoder with an affine decoder and nonlinear encoder, as an alternative representation for per-pixel distance losses in image restoration. Rather than modifying model architectures or auxiliary objectives, the authors replace standard RGB-space losses (L₁, L₂, smooth L₁) with their counterparts in aRGB space during training of deblurring, denoising, and super-resolution models. The core claim is that RGB is not optimal for image restoration and that shifting the loss computation to aRGB yields improved visual quality without architectural changes.

**Strengths:**
- **Simple and general approach**: The method is elegantly straightforward—training an autoencoder to define a new loss space and then redirecting existing losses requires minimal engineering. The generality across multiple restoration tasks (deblurring, denoising, super-resolution) is a practical advantage.
- **Addresses a real problem**: The observation that per-pixel RGB losses lead to blurry, unrealistic textures is well-established, and attempting to solve it at the representation level rather than via auxiliary losses is a reasonable direction.
- **No architectural overhead**: The approach does not require modifying restoration model architectures or tuning hyperparameters, making it broadly applicable.

**Weaknesses:**
- **Limited methodological novelty**: Learning a task-specific latent space via autoencoders is not new. The specific design (affine decoder + nonlinear encoder) is not well justified—why this particular architecture? How sensitive are results to this choice? The paper does not explain why this design preserves color while capturing low-level structures better than alternatives.
- **Insufficient experimental validation**: The abstract provides no quantitative results, comparison baselines, or ablation studies. Without seeing actual numbers, visual comparisons, or statistical significance tests, it is impossible to assess whether the claimed "boosted performance" is substantial or marginal. Which metrics improve (PSNR, SSIM, perceptual metrics)? By how much?
- **Unclear training procedure**: How is the aRGB autoencoder trained? On what data? Is it task-specific or universal? How does its training interact with restoration model training? These details are essential for reproducibility and understanding whether gains come from the representation or from additional training signal.
- **Missing ablations and analysis**: The paper lacks ablation studies (e.g., different encoder/decoder designs, comparison to other learned representations) and does not provide intuition for why aRGB should work better. What do the learned transformations look like? How do they differ from standard color spaces (e.g., YCbCr, Lab)?

**Questions:**
- How is the aRGB autoencoder trained, and is it task-specific or universal across restoration tasks?
- What are the quantitative improvements in standard metrics (PSNR, SSIM) and perceptual metrics across the three tasks?
- How sensitive are results to the autoencoder architecture? Have you tried other designs (e.g., deeper encoders, different decoder constraints)?
- How does aRGB compare to other learned or hand-crafted color spaces (Lab, YCbCr, etc.)?
- Can you provide ablations isolating the contribution of the affine decoder constraint versus the nonlinear encoder?

**Overall score:**
5: marginally below the acceptance threshold

The paper tackles a relevant problem and proposes a simple, general solution. However, the abstract lacks sufficient experimental evidence (no quantitative results, comparisons, or ablations are provided) to validate the claims. The methodological contribution is incremental—applying standard autoencoder learning to define a loss space—and the design choices are not well justified. For a venue like ICLR, the paper would need comprehensive experiments, ablations, and clearer technical justification to meet the acceptance bar.
