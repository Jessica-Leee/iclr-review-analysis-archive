**Review:**

This paper proposes PraX, a method to amortize pragmatic inference in program synthesis by training neural models on self-play-generated examples. The core idea is to sample program-example pairs via self-play between listener (synthesizer) and speaker (example generator) models, then use pragmatic inference to select informative training examples that help the synthesizer disambiguate user intent without human supervision. The method is validated on regular expression synthesis, where it achieves 23% absolute improvement (51% relative) over baselines trained without pragmatic example selection and matches supervised learning on human-provided pragmatic examples.

**Strengths:**
- **Novel amortization strategy**: The use of self-play to generate candidate examples and pragmatic inference to filter them is creative and addresses a real computational bottleneck in prior work that required expensive counterfactual reasoning over large program spaces.
- **Strong empirical results**: The reported improvements are substantial—matching human-supervised performance without any human data is a compelling validation of the approach. The 51% relative improvement over non-pragmatic baselines is significant.
- **Unsupervised training**: The method requires no human-annotated pragmatic examples, which is practically valuable and demonstrates that pragmatic principles can be learned from self-play alone.
- **Clear problem motivation**: The paper articulates well why prior pragmatic inference approaches are infeasible and why amortization is needed.

**Weaknesses:**
- **Limited experimental scope**: Evaluation is restricted to regular expression synthesis. While this is a challenging domain, the generalizability of PraX to other program synthesis tasks (e.g., string manipulation, list processing, or more complex domains) remains unclear. No ablation studies or analysis of which components of the method are critical.
- **Insufficient technical detail in abstract**: The abstract does not clearly explain how pragmatic inference is used to "choose informative training examples" from the self-play sample. The mechanism for filtering examples and its relationship to classical pragmatic inference (e.g., Rational Speech Acts) is underspecified.
- **Missing comparisons and baselines**: The paper compares against models trained without pragmatic examples, but lacks comparison to other active learning or example selection strategies that might achieve similar gains without the pragmatic framework.
- **Self-play convergence and stability**: No discussion of whether the listener and speaker models converge during self-play, how many iterations are needed, or whether the method is sensitive to initialization. Training dynamics are not analyzed.

**Questions:**
- How does performance scale with program space size? Does the method remain effective for more complex synthesis domains?
- What is the computational cost of self-play and pragmatic filtering compared to training on random examples?
- Can you provide ablations showing the contribution of pragmatic inference versus other components (e.g., self-play alone, or random filtering)?
- How sensitive is the method to hyperparameters (e.g., number of self-play iterations, sample size for pragmatic filtering)?

**Overall score:**
6: marginally above the acceptance threshold

The paper presents a novel and well-motivated approach to an important problem in program synthesis. The core idea of amortizing pragmatic inference via self-play is creative, and the empirical results on regex synthesis are strong. However, the evaluation is narrow (single domain), technical details are sparse, and the generalizability of the approach is uncertain. The work makes a solid contribution to the intersection of pragmatics and program synthesis, but would benefit from broader evaluation and deeper analysis of the method's components and failure modes.

**Confidence:**
3 (moderate)

The abstract provides sufficient information to assess the contribution and results, but lacks implementation details and experimental scope that would increase confidence in the method's broader applicability.

**Soundness:**
Good

The approach is conceptually sound—using self-play to generate examples and pragmatic inference to filter them is a reasonable way to amortize expensive counterfactual reasoning. However, without details on convergence, stability, and ablations, full soundness cannot be confirmed.

**Presentation:**
Good

The abstract is clear and well-written, with good motivation for the problem. The method name (PraX) is memorable. However, the technical mechanism for pragmatic filtering could be explained more precisely.

**Contribution:**
Good

The paper makes a solid contribution by proposing a practical way to leverage pragmatic inference in program synthesis without human supervision. The idea of self-play + pragmatic filtering is novel in this context, though the evaluation scope limits the impact.
