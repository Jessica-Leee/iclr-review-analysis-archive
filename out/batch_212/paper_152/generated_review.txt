**Review:**

This paper proposes a game-theoretic framework for trustworthy machine learning that formulates the problem as a multi-objective multi-agent optimization problem. The key contribution is the introduction of regulation games, exemplified by SpecGame, which models the interaction between an ML model builder and regulators seeking to enforce fairness and privacy constraints. To find socially optimal solutions on the Pareto frontier, the authors introduce ParetoPlay, an equilibrium search algorithm designed to avoid inefficiencies that arise when regulators do not proactively specify their requirements. The paper demonstrates the framework on a gender classification task, showing that passive regulation can lead to privacy guarantees 3.76× worse than ordained requirements.

**Strengths:**
- Novel perspective: Framing trustworthy ML as a multi-agent game between model builders and regulators is intuitive and addresses a real gap—most prior work treats trust aspects in isolation and ignores the distinction between those who build models and those who assess them.
- Practical motivation: The concrete example showing how regulatory passivity leads to significantly degraded privacy guarantees (3.76× worse) provides compelling policy-relevant insight.
- Principled approach: Using game theory and Pareto optimality to characterize socially efficient solutions is theoretically well-motivated and avoids the pitfalls of other equilibria.

**Weaknesses:**
- Limited empirical validation: The paper presents only one application (gender classification) with sparse experimental details. Broader evaluation across multiple domains, fairness/privacy trade-off scenarios, and baseline comparisons would strengthen claims about the framework's generality and practical utility.
- Incomplete algorithmic description: ParetoPlay is introduced as a "novel equilibrium search algorithm," but the abstract provides no details on its computational complexity, convergence guarantees, or how it scales. Without this information, it is unclear whether the approach is practical for realistic ML systems.
- Unclear scope and assumptions: The paper does not articulate key assumptions about the game structure (e.g., information asymmetries, commitment mechanisms, or whether agents are rational). The relationship between the abstract game formulation and the specific SpecGame instance needs clarification.
- Missing related work discussion: The abstract does not position the work relative to existing multi-objective optimization, mechanism design, or game-theoretic approaches to fairness and privacy, making it difficult to assess novelty and impact.

**Questions:**
- How does ParetoPlay compare computationally to standard multi-objective optimization algorithms (e.g., scalarization, evolutionary methods)?
- Can you provide convergence guarantees or sample complexity bounds for ParetoPlay?
- How sensitive are the results to assumptions about agent rationality and information structure?
- How does the framework extend to settings with more than two agents or asymmetric information?

**Overall score:**
6: marginally above the acceptance threshold

The paper tackles an important and underexplored problem—holistic trustworthy ML through multi-agent game theory—with a principled approach. The core insight that regulators and model builders have distinct roles and incentives is valuable for policy. However, the work is held back by limited empirical validation (single application), insufficient algorithmic detail (ParetoPlay lacks complexity/convergence analysis), and incomplete positioning relative to related work. With more comprehensive experiments, clearer algorithmic exposition, and broader evaluation, this could be a strong contribution. As presented, it is a promising direction that merits publication but needs strengthening in execution and validation.
