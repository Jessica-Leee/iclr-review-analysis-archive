**Review:**

This paper proposes SASANet, a self-interpreting neural network that integrates Shapley values into an Additive Self-Attribution (ASA) framework. The core contribution is a method that provides theoretical guarantees that self-attribution values equal the output's Shapley values, achieved through a marginal contribution-based sequential schema and internal distillation-based training. The authors claim their approach avoids the expressiveness-interpretability trade-off that plagues existing self-interpreting models and outperforms both post-hoc interpretation methods and competing self-attributing architectures.

**Strengths:**
- The motivation is clear: grounding self-attribution in Shapley values provides a principled, theoretically justified foundation for interpretability, addressing a real gap in the literature.
- The claimed theoretical guarantee that self-attribution values equal Shapley values is a strong contribution if rigorously proven; this would distinguish SASANet from approximation-based methods.
- The paper positions itself as maintaining competitive predictive performance while providing intrinsic interpretability, which is an important practical consideration.
- The comparison against both post-hoc methods and self-attributing baselines is appropriate for validating the approach.

**Weaknesses:**
- The abstract provides insufficient detail on the actual mechanism: how exactly does the "marginal contribution-based sequential schema" work? How does "internal distillation-based training" ensure Shapley value alignment? Without clarity on these core technical components, it is difficult to assess feasibility or novelty.
- No concrete experimental results are presented in the abstract—claims like "surpasses existing self-attributing models" and "rivals black-box models" lack supporting numbers, making it impossible to evaluate the empirical contribution.
- The scalability of computing Shapley values is notoriously challenging; the abstract does not address computational complexity or how the method scales to high-dimensional inputs, which is critical for practical applicability.
- The paper lacks discussion of how the method handles the fundamental tension between maintaining un-approximated Shapley values and training efficiency, or what trade-offs (if any) are made.

**Questions:**
- Can you provide the theoretical proof that the self-attribution values provably equal Shapley values? What are the assumptions?
- How does computational cost scale with the number of features? Are there approximations or pruning strategies employed in practice?
- What are the specific performance metrics and datasets used in experiments? How large are the improvements over baselines?
- How does the method perform on high-dimensional datasets (e.g., images, text), where Shapley value computation is typically intractable?

5: marginally below the acceptance threshold

The core claims—theoretical guarantees and empirical superiority—cannot be properly assessed without concrete results and clearer exposition of the method.
