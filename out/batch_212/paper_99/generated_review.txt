**Review:**

This paper addresses an important practical problem: how to perform knowledge distillation when teacher model inferences are expensive and limited. The authors propose Comparative Knowledge Distillation (CKD), which learns from pairwise or group-wise comparisons of the teacher's predictions rather than absolute outputs. The key insight is that by encouraging the student to match the teacher's relative ordering or differences across samples, CKD extracts additional learning signals without requiring additional teacher calls. The method is motivated by educational principles and extends naturally from pairs to groups of samples.

**Strengths:**

- **Well-motivated problem:** The Few-Teacher-Inference Knowledge Distillation (FTI-KD) setting is timely and practically relevant given the prevalence of expensive proprietary models.
- **Elegant core idea:** Learning from comparative signals (relative differences) rather than absolute predictions is intuitive and theoretically sound—it reuses a fixed set of teacher outputs more efficiently.
- **No additional teacher overhead:** CKD provides richer learning signals without increasing the number of teacher inferences, which directly addresses the stated constraint.
- **Generality:** The extension from pairwise to group-wise comparisons suggests a flexible framework applicable across different model architectures and domains.

**Weaknesses:**

- **Limited methodological detail in abstract:** The abstract does not specify how comparative losses are formulated (e.g., ranking losses, contrastive objectives, or other mechanisms). Without seeing the full paper, it is unclear whether the technical contribution is substantial or primarily a straightforward application of existing comparison-based learning techniques.
- **Unclear experimental scope:** The abstract mentions "varied experimental settings" and claims consistent outperformance of "state-of-the-art data augmentation and KD techniques," but provides no concrete details on datasets, baselines, or magnitude of improvements. This makes it difficult to assess the practical significance of the results.
- **Missing ablation and analysis:** There is no discussion of which components of CKD (pairwise vs. group-wise, loss design, hyperparameter sensitivity) drive performance, or how performance scales with the number of teacher inferences available.
- **Comparison fairness:** It is unclear whether baselines are given equal computational budgets or whether they are adapted to the FTI-KD setting. Standard KD and data augmentation methods may not be designed for this constraint, making direct comparison potentially unfair.

**Questions:**

- How is the comparative loss formulated? Is it a ranking loss (e.g., triplet loss, ListNet), contrastive loss, or something else?
- What is the magnitude of improvement over baselines, and on which datasets? Are improvements consistent across vision and NLP tasks?
- How does performance degrade as the number of teacher inferences decreases further (e.g., 10× fewer calls)?
- Have you compared against other sample-efficient KD methods, such as those using synthetic data generation or active learning?
