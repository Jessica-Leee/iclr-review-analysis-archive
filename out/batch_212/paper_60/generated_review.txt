**Review:**

This paper studies the loss landscape of two-layer mildly overparameterized ReLU networks on finite datasets, using the rank of the Jacobian of the parametrization map to bound the dimension of sets of local and global minima. The main contributions are: (1) showing that most activation patterns correspond to parameter regions with no bad differentiable local minima, leveraging results on random binary matrices; and (2) proving that for one-dimensional input data, most activation regions contain high-dimensional global minima sets and no bad local minima. The authors experimentally validate a phase transition in Jacobian rank depending on the degree of overparameterization.

**Strengths:**
- The use of Jacobian rank to characterize the geometry of minima is a principled and elegant approach that provides new geometric insights into the loss landscape.
- The theoretical results are non-trivial, particularly the connection to random binary matrices and the characterization of activation patterns, which offers a fresh perspective on why overparameterized networks generalize well.
- The paper addresses an important problem—understanding when neural networks have favorable loss landscapes—which is central to optimization and generalization theory.
- Experimental validation of the phase transition provides concrete evidence supporting the theoretical predictions.

**Weaknesses:**
- The restriction to two-layer networks with one-dimensional input data for the strongest results (high-dimensional global minima, no bad local minima) significantly limits practical relevance. Most modern applications involve deeper networks and higher-dimensional data.
- The notion of "mildly overparameterized" is not precisely defined in the abstract, making it unclear how the results scale with network width and dataset size. The relationship between overparameterization degree and the favorable landscape properties needs clarification.
- The paper lacks discussion of how these landscape properties translate to actual optimization dynamics and generalization performance. Understanding the loss landscape is valuable, but the practical implications for training and test error remain unclear.
- Limited experimental validation: the experiments appear to focus primarily on the phase transition phenomenon rather than comprehensive empirical validation on realistic datasets or comparison with other landscape analysis approaches.

**Questions:**
- How does the result generalize beyond two-layer networks? Is there a clear barrier to extending the Jacobian rank approach to deeper architectures?
- Can you provide a precise definition of "mildly overparameterized" and characterize how the favorable landscape properties degrade as overparameterization decreases?
- Do the theoretical guarantees on the absence of bad local minima have implications for convergence rates of standard optimizers like SGD?
- How do the results change for other loss functions beyond squared error, or for classification tasks?

**Overall score:**
6: marginally above the acceptance threshold

The paper makes solid theoretical contributions to understanding neural network loss landscapes through a novel Jacobian rank perspective, with supporting experiments. However, the restriction to two-layer networks and one-dimensional inputs for the strongest results, combined with limited clarity on practical implications and scalability, prevents this from being a strong accept. The work is technically sound and addresses an important question, but the gap between theory (1D case) and practice (high-dimensional, deep networks) is substantial.
