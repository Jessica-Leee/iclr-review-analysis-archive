**Review:**

This paper establishes a theoretical framework connecting loss function symmetries to structured constraints in neural network learning. The core contribution is proving that every mirror symmetry of the loss function induces a structured constraint that becomes favored under large weight decay or gradient noise. The authors derive concrete corollaries—rescaling symmetry → sparsity, rotation symmetry → low rankness, permutation symmetry → homogeneous ensembling—and claim the framework explains phenomena like loss of plasticity and neural collapse. The work aims to provide a unifying perspective on why common architectural symmetries lead to specific inductive biases.

**Strengths:**

1. **Novel theoretical perspective**: The connection between loss symmetries and structured constraints is conceptually clean and potentially impactful. Framing common phenomena (sparsity, low-rank solutions, ensembling) as consequences of symmetry-induced constraints is elegant and offers a unifying lens.

2. **Concrete mathematical claims**: The paper makes specific, testable claims (e.g., rescaling symmetry → sparsity) with stated proofs, which is valuable for a theory-focused contribution.

3. **Breadth of application**: The framework addresses multiple important phenomena (plasticity loss, neural collapse, homogeneous ensembling), suggesting generality.

**Weaknesses:**

1. **Insufficient empirical validation**: The abstract provides no details on experiments, datasets, or quantitative validation. For a paper claiming to "explain" real phenomena, the lack of empirical evidence in the abstract is concerning. It is unclear whether predictions are validated on standard benchmarks or toy problems.

2. **Clarity and accessibility**: The abstract is dense and assumes familiarity with the framework. Key definitions (what constitutes a "mirror symmetry," how "structured constraints" are formally defined, the precise conditions under which they become "favored") are not explained. Without access to the full paper, it is difficult to assess rigor.

3. **Limited novelty in individual results**: While the unifying framework is interesting, the individual corollaries (e.g., rescaling → sparsity) may be known or relatively straightforward. The novelty hinges on whether the general theory is truly new or a repackaging of existing observations.

4. **Gap between theory and practice**: The conditions for constraints to be "favored" (large weight decay or gradient noise) may not reflect typical training regimes. It is unclear how well the theory predicts behavior in standard deep learning settings without these assumptions.

**Questions:**

- What is the formal definition of a "mirror symmetry" and how does it relate to standard notions of symmetry in mathematics?
- Are the theoretical predictions validated empirically on standard datasets (e.g., CIFAR-10, ImageNet)? What is the quantitative agreement?
- How do the results change when weight decay or gradient noise are moderate or small—the typical case in practice?
- Can the framework predict or prevent neural collapse in specific architectures, or is it primarily post-hoc explanation?

**Overall score:**

6: marginally above the acceptance threshold

The paper presents an interesting theoretical framework with potential to unify understanding of neural network behavior. However, the abstract lacks sufficient empirical validation and clarity to fully assess the contribution. If the full paper provides rigorous proofs, comprehensive experiments, and clear practical implications, this could be a solid accept. As presented, the work is promising but needs stronger empirical grounding and clearer exposition to be compelling.
