**Review:**

This paper evaluates the generalization capacity of neural network architectures on multimodal reasoning tasks, introducing a configurable benchmark (gCOG) that tests three forms of out-of-distribution generalization: distractor generalization, systematic compositional generalization, and productive compositional generalization. The authors compare multiple architectures (RNNs, standard Transformers, Perceiver, and others) and find that cross-attention mechanisms—particularly in models like the Perceiver—improve performance on distractor and systematic generalization, while all tested architectures fail on productive compositional generalization. The work provides a structured empirical study with a reusable benchmark for future research.

**Strengths:**
- **Clear problem formulation and taxonomy**: The paper articulates three distinct types of multimodal OOD generalization with intuitive definitions, providing a useful conceptual framework for the community.
- **Comprehensive architectural comparison**: Systematic evaluation across multiple base architectures (RNNs, Transformers, Perceiver) with controlled comparisons helps isolate the role of cross-attention mechanisms.
- **Reusable benchmark contribution**: gCOG is presented as a configurable benchmark with multiple splits, which could facilitate future research on multimodal generalization.
- **Honest reporting of failures**: The paper transparently reports that all architectures fail on productive compositional generalization, highlighting genuine limitations rather than overstating positive results.

**Weaknesses:**
- **Limited novelty in methodology**: The paper is primarily an empirical evaluation study without introducing new architectures, training procedures, or theoretical insights. The benchmark, while useful, is largely a structured evaluation protocol rather than a fundamental methodological advance.
- **Insufficient analysis of why cross-attention helps**: While the paper observes that cross-attention improves performance on some tasks, it provides limited mechanistic insight into *why* this occurs or what properties of cross-attention are critical. The analysis remains largely descriptive.
- **Incomplete coverage of modern architectures**: The paper does not evaluate more recent multimodal models (e.g., CLIP-based approaches, vision-language models, or other contemporary cross-modal architectures), limiting the relevance of conclusions for current practice.
- **Lack of theoretical grounding**: The paper offers no formal analysis or theoretical framework explaining why productive compositional generalization is fundamentally harder, or what architectural properties might address this limitation.
- **Missing implementation and reproducibility details**: The abstract does not clarify dataset sizes, hyperparameter selection, number of runs, statistical significance testing, or code availability, raising questions about reproducibility and robustness of findings.

**Questions:**
- How were hyperparameters tuned for each architecture? Were they equally optimized, or could performance differences reflect tuning effort rather than architectural capacity?
- Can the authors provide ablation studies isolating which components of cross-attention (e.g., query-key interaction, value aggregation) drive improvements on distractor and systematic tasks?
- Why were recent vision-language models excluded? How do findings generalize to pre-trained multimodal systems?
- What is the statistical significance of performance differences across architectures, and how sensitive are results to random seeds?

**Overall score:**
5: marginally below the acceptance threshold

The paper addresses a relevant question and provides a useful benchmark, but the contribution is primarily empirical without sufficient novelty in methodology, architecture, or theoretical insight. The analysis of *why* certain mechanisms work remains shallow, and the omission of contemporary multimodal models limits impact. While the honest reporting of failures and the structured taxonomy are commendable, the work reads more as a thorough empirical study than a significant advance in understanding or capability. For a top-tier venue like ICLR, stronger mechanistic insights, theoretical analysis, or novel architectural proposals would be expected.

**Confidence:** 3

**Soundness:** Fair

The experimental design appears reasonable, but critical details on hyperparameter tuning, statistical testing, and reproducibility are absent from the abstract. The conclusions about cross-attention are supported by the reported results, but the lack of ablations and deeper analysis limits confidence in the mechanistic claims.

**Presentation:** Good

The paper is clearly written with well-defined problem categories and a coherent narrative. The taxonomy of generalization types is intuitive and well-articulated.

**Contribution:** Fair

The benchmark (gCOG) is a useful community resource, and the systematic comparison provides value. However, the lack of novel methods, architectures, or theoretical insights limits the contribution to primarily an empirical evaluation study.
